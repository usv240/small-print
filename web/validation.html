Validation

What we know, and how we know it

Every claim about Small Print falls into one of four kinds of evidence, and we label which. Small Print has not been validated in a field study with real participants. We say exactly what has been measured, what has been checked against published references, what has only been simulated, and what still needs to be done.

At a glance

  • Refusals (negative controls): 0 distances invented from 2,694 frames with no face and 0 from 907 frames with covered eyes (sunglasses, a hand), while real eyes are accepted 100% of the time on the bench and on 353 of 357 photos of 51 real people; 0 readers recommended for short-sight patterns or outside +1.00 to +3.00 across 612,732 checked inputs.
  • Real patients: on 296 people from two published clinical datasets, Small Print's starting strength matched the clinician within ±0.50 D for 86.5%, the same as the clinical age table (84.1%), and it withheld readers from 12 of 23 short-sighted patients that the table would have given them to.
  • Real faces: on 51 real people photographed by Caltech at measured distances, the app's face model found every face (357/357), and the implied iris size was 11.1 ± 0.6 mm, close to the published 11.7 ± 0.5 mm that the distance method relies on.
  • Measured: the camera distance pipeline has a mean error of 0.34% (largest 1.1%) from 22 to 70 cm, over 2,803 frames on a bench.
  • Verified: 87 automated tests (optics, recommendation, API and 11 end-to-end), all passing, with 1 documented expected failure.
  • Simulated: across 20,000 virtual people, Small Print + the try-on makes the right call 84.9% of the time, against 42.0% for the age table and 50.9–52.1% for a rack card.
  • Real data: the strengths our virtual people need match programmes in Kenya (31,474 people), India and Eritrea within about 0.2 D.
  • Not done yet: no field validation with real participants. A 200-person pilot against a trained screener is proposed.

A tool that picks reading glasses must also know when not to answer. We measured that directly, as the first result rather than a footnote.

How we found and fixed the covered-eye case. With both eyes hidden, the face model still places iris points where the eyes should be, and the app accepted a distance from them. We added an eye-visibility check: for each eye, the centre (iris and pupil) must be clearly darker than the ring around it. Real eyes score 0.45–0.87 on the bench (median 0.69 on 51 real people), covered eyes −0.08 to 0.12. The threshold is 0.16, lowered from 0.25 after the real-people check below so that people with light irises aren't refused. Brightness spread alone didn't work: a hand overlapped real eyes in dim light. Code: distance.ts.

0c. Real patients: two published clinical datasets Real data

We ran the app's recommendation code, unchanged, on per-person clinical records we did not collect: a south Indian eye clinic (Khurana et al., Scientific Reports 2023, n = 342)18 and a Madrid study (García-del-Castillo et al., 2026, n = 30)19, both CC BY 4.0. Each record has the clinic's measured near point, the person's age and the reading prescription a clinician chose. The near points come from the clinics, not from our camera, so this tests the decision logic, not the camera.

What this means, plainly.

  • Accuracy is the same, not better. On strength alone, Small Print's starting estimate is as accurate as the clinical age table on real patients. The difference (86.5% vs 84.1%) is not statistically significant (p = 0.28).
  • The real-data advantage is refusals. It withheld readers from 12 of 23 short-sighted patients, who would be harmed by them. The age table offered readers to all 23.
  • The second real dataset confirms our simulation's warning. The starting estimate runs about 0.2–0.3 D weak, matching what we saw against the Kenya data. That is why the app always offers the shop check, which uses real lenses. No public dataset lets us test that step on real people, so it remains the main thing a field pilot must measure.
  • Long-sighted people needing more than +3.00 (n = 42) were missed by both methods. Only the shop check can catch them.
  • The short-sight check can't fire for people under about 46 who are only mildly short-sighted: their closest sharp point is nearer than a phone camera can measure. We disclose this rather than claim coverage.

1. Measured: the camera distance pipeline Measured

Distance pipeline: bench measurement

Measured Bench measurement: a real face photo, rescaled to simulate distance, through the real MediaPipe pipeline in Chrome on a Windows laptop. This is not a physical ruler test on phones or laptops.

Method: a face photo rescaled to 22–70 cm, run through the shipped pipeline in Chrome

Method. In a pinhole camera, moving a face from distance d to d/s makes its image s times bigger. So drawing the same face photo s times bigger is, for the camera, the same face s times closer. We took MediaPipe's public test portraita and drew it at 10 sizes on a 640×480 frame, equivalent to 22–70 cm. Chrome played each frame as its webcam (its built-in fake camera), and the measurement lab ran Small Print's shipped code unchanged: MediaPipe Face Landmarker, then iris width, then distance. For each size we opened 3 fresh browser sessions, waited 2.5 s for the face tracker to settle, then recorded every frame for 3 s: 2,803 frames in all. The 30 cm size stands in for the app's one-time 30 cm camera calibration; every other size is then read as distance and compared with its true relative distance. Error = (camera − true) ÷ true. Code: web/bench-camera/. Raw frames: camera-bench.json.

What it shows. After a single 30 cm calibration, the pipeline turns image size into distance correctly across the whole reading range: a straight-line fit of camera against true distance has a slope of 1.000. The three sessions at each distance agreed to within 0.07 percentage points, so these small errors are systematic rather than random. The largest, −1.1% at 22 cm, is where the face nearly fills the frame and the photo had to be enlarged 1.4 times. Landmark jitter on an unchanging image was at most 0.26 mm per frame, before the app's own smoothing.

Speed, what the bench does not show (real faces, real phones), and how to read the error

Speed. MediaPipe loaded its GPU (WebGL) path on the laptop's Intel Arc graphics (Intel Core Ultra 9 185H, headless Chrome 153); no fallback to the CPU path was logged. It kept up with the fake camera's full 30 fps: 95% of one-second readings were 30 or 31. Fed 60 fps instead, it processed a median of 43 fps (39–46). Three of the 30 sessions had brief dips (lowest one-second reading 8 fps) while other jobs were running on the laptop. Phones will be slower.

What it does not show.

  • Absolute accuracy without calibration. A photo has no real distance, so the uncalibrated estimate (which assumes a 70° camera field of view) can't be checked this way. We chose the 30 cm photo size so the uncalibrated reading was also 30 cm, so agreement there is by construction.
  • Real faces and real conditions. One face, frontal, evenly lit and perfectly still. Head movement, turning, glasses, dim light and different faces all add error. A flat photo also can't reproduce the small perspective changes of a real head; for the nearly flat iris that effect is small.
  • Real devices. One Windows laptop, a fake camera, no phone. Google's own evaluation of iris-based distance on real people found a 4.3% mean relative error (SD 2.4%) against an iPhone 11 depth sensor, over 200+ participants.b That is a published result, not ours, and it is the better guide to real-world accuracy.

A physical ruler check on our own Pixel and laptop, using the measurement lab at 25, 40 and 60 cm, will be added to the table above if it is completed before the deadline.

  1. MediaPipe test portrait, storage.googleapis.com/mediapipe-assets/portrait.jpg (an official portrait used in MediaPipe's own tests; downloaded at run time, not redistributed).
  2. Google Research. MediaPipe Iris: real-time iris tracking and depth estimation. 2020. research.google/blog/mediapipe-iris-real-time-iris-tracking-depth-estimation

How to read it. Demand is 1/distance, so a distance error of e% changes the demand by about e% of its value. At 40 cm (2.50 D), 4% is 0.10 D and 10% is 0.25 D. Reader strengths are usually stocked 0.50 D apart. The bench's largest error, 1.1% at 22 cm (4.5 D), is 0.05 D.

How big a CSS pixel really is on common phones and laptops, computed from published specs, is on Findings.

3. Simulated: 20,000 virtual people Simulation

This is a simulation, not clinical validation. Every "person" is a set of numbers drawn from published age norms and stated assumptions. No real participant was tested. The bench shows whether the algorithm is internally consistent and how it degrades under plausible noise. It cannot show how accurate the app is on real eyes.

The bench calls Small Print's own functions unchanged on simulated, noisy measurements, and compares them with four other ways of choosing readers. Each method's choice is scored against the person's simulated ideal strength: the one that puts their reading distance in the middle of their clear range.5 Run: bench 1.0.0, core sha256 345c07e2c4e5, seed 20261003, generated 2026-10-01T21:47:23.890Z, reproducible to the last digit. Full method, equations and code: web/sim on GitHub. Raw numbers: bench.json.

The virtual population: 10,763 in scope for ready-made readers (ideal +0.75 to +3.00), 3,233 who don't need readers yet, 2,036 who should be referred for short sight (worse than −1.00 D), and 3,968 who need more than +3.00 and should be referred.

How the other methods are modelled, and what the stronger-pair check adds

"Rack card" is modelled as an ideal blur meter whose reserve was tuned to maximise the card's own score, which is deliberately generous to the card. The age table and rack card never ask about short sight, so they hand readers to nearly every short-sighted person and to everyone who doesn't need them yet.

The stronger-pair check. The highlighted row is what ships, including the step described on How it works: when a pair is still sharp at arm's length, so the far end of the clear range can't be measured, the app asks you to also try one pair 0.50 stronger, and to keep the first pair if that one is too strong. The rows without the check are kept for comparison. The check adds about 1 point within ±0.25 D with 0.50 stock (72.9% vs 71.8%) and about 3 points with 0.25 stock (76.7% vs 74.0%), at the cost of trying more pairs (1.88 on average with 0.50 stock, against 1.30 without it). In bench.json and the bench summary the shipped check still carries its earlier label, "try-on + probe pair". Estimating the far end from the lens power instead was tried and rejected: in this bench it is less accurate for people who need readers (69.6% / 81.6%) and tells more of them they don't need readers yet (7.9% vs 4.8%), even though it hands readers too early to far fewer people (4.3%).

Where Small Print is not better

"After the try-on" here means the shipped try-on with the stronger-pair check, 0.50 stock (its breakdowns by age and refractive error are in bench.json).

  • Ages 60–70: the plain age table wins on in-scope accuracy. Within ±0.50 D, the 40 cm table is right for 92.3% (60–64) and 94.8% (65–70) of in-scope people, against 83.3% and 84.7% for Small Print's start and 91.0% and 91.1% after the try-on. In this model almost everyone over about 62 has no focusing left, so +2.50 suits most of them. Over everyone (right call), the start alone also loses to the rack card at 60+ (60.1–61.2% vs 66.8–72.8%); after the try-on Small Print leads (86.3–87.3%).
  • Ages 40–44: Small Print says "not yet" to many borderline people. The table's +1.00 is within ±0.50 D for 73.5% of in-scope people, against about 45% for Small Print (45.7% start, 44.0% after try-on), because Small Print tells many people whose ideal is +0.75 or +1.00 that they don't need readers yet. Over everyone it is the other way round: 78.1–81.0% right call against 30.8%, because the table gives readers to everyone.
  • Uncorrected long sight is a weak spot for every method. For in-scope people with +0.76 to +2.00 D of long sight, the try-on is within ±0.25 D for only 37.1% (bias −0.44 D, too weak), worse than the age table's 49.6%. Above +2.00 D almost nobody is within ±0.50 D by any method. A test done only up close can't tell long sight apart from lost focusing.
  • Arm-stretch is the biggest real-world risk. If people hold the phone halfway out toward their no-glasses near point when asked where they read, the try-on falls to 39.3% within ±0.25 D and 60.1% within ±0.50 D (bias −0.47 D), below the age table (49.4% / 75.1%). This is why the app says "not stretched out" and limits the reading distance to 25–45 cm. Whether real people stretch is unknown until the pilot.
  • The far end of the clear range is often out of reach. In 52.6% of simulated try-on measurements, the pair was still sharp at arm's length. On its own, the try-on can then only say "good" or "stronger"; that is why the app asks for one pair 0.50 stronger. With perfect measurements it reaches 80.9% within ±0.25 D (78.0% without the stronger-pair check); with the far end always measurable and no refractive error, 99.3%.
  • The short-sight check catches only some short-sighted people at the start. 33.0% of them are still given a starting strength; after the try-on, 11.1%.

  10. Camera distance error: 4% shared per person, plus 1% per reading.7 Size from MediaPipe Iris; the split is assumed.
  11. Error judging where the E blurs: 0.25 D per judgement. Assumption (swept 0–0.5 D).
  12. At the rack: try the weaker of the two suggested pairs first, follow the advice, stop at "good" or after 3 pairs. Assumption about how people use the screen.
  13. Rack card: an ideal blur meter with a reserve tuned in the card's favour; letter-size changes with distance are ignored. Assumption, generous to the card.
  14. No learning, fatigue or lighting effects; everyone follows the instructions. Assumption.

What matched

  • The strengths people need. For people who need readers, the power our virtual people need at each age is within about 0.2 D of the real programmes on average (0.16 D vs Kenya, 0.17 D vs India; within 0.03 D of Kenya at 60+).
  • The overall spread of strengths overlaps Kenya's distribution by 74–80%.

What didn't, and what we did about it

  • Our virtual people need reading glasses too late. In Asmara, 77.6% of 41–45-year-olds had presbyopia; in our simulation almost none do at that age. We rebuilt the simulated population to match the Asmara data and re-ran everything (table below).
  • Too many virtual people need more than +3.00 (15.3% vs 5.9% in Kenya). We re-weighted them to Kenya's share and re-ran.
  • Small Print suggests slightly weaker pairs than Kenyan programme staff handed out: 0.30–0.52 D weaker in every age band in simulation (0.02–0.36 D vs the Indian optometrist). Part is by design: it refers anyone above +3.00 and starts with the weaker of two pairs. Part is that our simulated people need less at ages 45–54. We have not re-tuned the algorithm to close this gap: tuning against our own simulation would be circular. It is the first thing a field pilot must measure.

4. Not done yet: field validation Not done

Nobody has yet compared Small Print's result with a trained screener's result on the same real people. Until that is done, nobody (including us) knows how accurate it is in practice. This is the study we propose:

Proposed pilot: 200 adults, a masked trained screener with trial lenses, primary outcome within ±0.50 D study design
Setting
A community vision screening event, run with a partner that already provides reading glasses.
Participants
200 adults aged 40 or over with near-vision difficulty. People with warning signs are referred as usual and counted.
Reference
A trained screener chooses the reader strength with trial lenses, following the WHO Training in Assistive Products / Peek approach,911 without seeing Small Print's result.
Small Print
Each person takes the test on a provided phone (and their own phone where possible), including the try-on with the screener's stock. Order randomised.
Primary outcome
Share of people whose Small Print strength is within ±0.50 D of the screener's.
Secondary
Within ±0.25 D and exact; start versus after try-on; agreement on who should be referred; time taken; finishing without help; results by age band, literacy, device and calibration; camera distance against a tape measure.
Precision
With 200 people, a result near 85% has a 95% confidence interval of about ±5 percentage points.
Ethics
Ethics approval and informed consent before any data collection; analysis plan registered in advance; all results published, including failures.
