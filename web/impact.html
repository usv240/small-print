The problem

1.8 billionpeople had presbyopia in 20151
826 millionhad near-vision impairment because it was not corrected1
~90%of people affected by uncorrected refractive error and presbyopia live in low- and middle-income countries2
1.2 millionpeople per optometrist in central sub-Saharan Africa2

A 2020 estimate puts the number of people aged 50 and over with near-vision impairment from uncorrected presbyopia at 510 million.1 The fix is cheap: in the PROSPER trial, reading glasses cost about US$1.80 a pair.3 The gap is access, and it is extremely unequal:

Effective near-vision refractive error coverage, 2021: roughly, the share of adults aged 50 and over who need near-vision correction and have correction that works for them.2

When people do get reading glasses, two randomised trials found large effects. Tea pickers in Assam, India, given reading glasses were 22% more productive (over 30% for workers over 50).3 In Bangladesh, people in near-vision-intensive work given reading glasses saw their income rise 33.4% more than those who weren't; only 35% of participants could read.4 These trials measured the effect of giving people glasses, not of using this app.

Why people were referred or advised

Live data appears here when JavaScript runs.

One screening can have several reasons, so these don't add up to the referral count. "Possible short sight" leads to "no readers" with an exam suggested; "estimates disagree" leads to a try-on suggestion. Urgent warning signs (sudden change, flashes, pain, redness) stop the test before any result exists, so they never appear here.

Agreement with readers people already own

Live data appears here when JavaScript runs.

After the result, people can tell us the strength printed on readers they already own. We compare it with Small Print's suggestion: the same strength, within 0.50, or further apart. This is a real-world signal, not validation: people's existing readers may not be right for them either, and the numbers are small.

How we count

  • One record per test. Each test gets a random ID. If the person later checks a pair or tells us about their existing readers, the same record is updated, so nobody is counted twice.
  • Four kinds of traffic, kept apart. Development: our own testing (local machines, or a ?dev link). Judge: anyone who opened a ?judge link in that browser tab. Demo: the slider instead of the camera. Real: everyone else, using the camera on the public site.
  • Anonymous by design. No names, images, IP addresses or exact ages; see exactly what is stored.
  • What these numbers are not. A count of screenings is not a count of people helped. We don't know whether anyone bought or wore readers afterwards.

Projection, not a measured outcome Projection

This is arithmetic on assumptions, not a measured outcome. The trials below measured what happened when people were given reading glasses. Nobody has measured what happens when people use Small Print. Each step is shown so you can change any assumption you disagree with.

Start with 1,000 people who need ready-made readers and take the test.

StepAssumptionWhere it comes fromPer 1,000
A. Pointed to a suitable pair (within ±0.50 D)85.0%Our simulation of Small Print + try-on with the stronger-pair check Simulation. Not measured on real people.1,000 × 0.850 = 850
B. Get the pair and use it88.3%THRIVE: self-reported use after 8 months among people given free glasses.4 Buying is a bigger step than being given, so this is optimistic.850 × 0.883 ≈ 751
C1. Productivity+22% eachPROSPER, tea pickers in Assam.3751 × 0.22 ≈ 165
C2. Income+33.4% eachTHRIVE, near-vision-intensive work in Bangladesh.4751 × 0.334 ≈ 251

How to read C1 and C2. If all 751 people did work like the trial participants, their combined extra output would equal the output of about 164 additional workers (PROSPER-like work), or their combined extra income would equal the income of about 249 additional earners (THRIVE-like work). These are two alternative readings, not a sum.

Why this could be wrong, in both directions:

  • Step A comes from a simulation. Real accuracy is unknown until the field pilot.
  • In THRIVE, 88.3% reported using their glasses, but only 57.3% wore them at least four days a week.4
  • THRIVE's income effect was analysed by intention to treat: it already averages over everyone offered glasses, including people who didn't wear them.4 Applying an uptake factor on top counts non-use twice, so this projection leans low on that point.
  • The effects were measured in specific jobs that depend on close-up work. Many people who need readers don't do such work, and effects may differ elsewhere or fade over time.

Path to scale

Small Print is free for individuals and will stay free. The plan is for organisations that sell or distribute reading glasses to pay for, or adopt, versions that fit their setting. We have no partners yet; these are the kinds of organisations the product is designed for.

Reader retailers

A white-label version of the test and the in-store try-on check. The value we expect for a retailer is fewer wrong-strength purchases and returns. That is a hypothesis to test with a retailer, not a measured result.

Pharmacy racks

A QR code on the rack opens the test on the customer's own phone, and the try-on check works right there at the rack. A shared kiosk mode is planned, not built.

Employer vision days

PROSPER's model was a workplace one: give near-work employees reading glasses.3 Queen's University Belfast, which led the trial, reports that this workplace approach has since reached about 750,000 workers.14 Small Print could help staff choose strengths before the event.

NGO and health-worker camps

Free for NGOs. Trained community health workers already provide reading glasses outside doctors' offices under the WHO's assistive-products training.11 A camp mode (many people on one device, working offline) is planned, not built.

Running cost. About US$0.59 per 1,000 screenings, almost all of it delivering the on-device face model and runtime (about 6.6 MB per first visit). AWS's CloudFront free tier (1 TB a month) covers roughly 150,000 screenings a month. The full cost table and assumptions are on Built on AWS.
